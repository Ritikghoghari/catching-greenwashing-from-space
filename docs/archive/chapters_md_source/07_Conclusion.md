# Chapter 7: Conclusion

## 7.1 Summary of findings

This thesis set out to check whether palm oil companies' public zero-deforestation claims hold up against independent satellite evidence. Across 11 companies and 290 matched mills, the Hansen Global Forest Change dataset records 890,168 hectares of forest loss inside 10 km mill buffers since the EUDR cutoff of 31 December 2020 (Section 4.2). All 11 companies in the dataset make deforestation-related claims in their sustainability reports, and every one of the 290 matched mills shows measurable post-2020 loss within its buffer, with the smallest single-mill total still recording 105 ha (Section 4.2, Section 5.3). No company and no mill in this sample is clean by the satellite record alone.

Against that shared baseline, the four-component mismatch score (forest loss, claim specificity, sentiment, and spatial severity, Section 4.3) separates the 11 companies by how well their public language corresponds to what satellite data shows near their mills. KLK ranks highest, at 65.6, driven by a maximum specificity score built from a small 8-claim sample. GAR ranks second, at 63.1, combining the largest total loss in the dataset, 171,236 ha across 50 mills, with the most consistently positive claim language of any company. Astra Agro ranks lowest, at 17.6, not because its loss is small, 87,198 ha, the fifth-largest total in the dataset, but because its claims are few, vague, and flatly worded, giving the satellite record little to contradict (Section 4.6, Section 5.4). No company scored near zero and none scored near the maximum. The gap between claim and evidence in this sector is a gradient across all 11 companies, not a split between a few bad actors and an otherwise clean industry (Section 5.1).

## 7.2 The gap this thesis filled

The literature reviewed in Chapter 2 identifies a specific, named gap. Calamai et al. (2026) find that no dataset of verified greenwashing cases exists, so text classifiers can be trained to detect vague language but not evaluated against ground truth of who actually greenwashed. Ong et al. (2025) find the same gap from the tool side: existing automated greenwashing detectors score text for vagueness or sentiment but do not check any stated claim against an independent, non-textual data source. Neither paper proposes or builds a tool that closes this gap for a specific sector.

This thesis closes it for palm oil. It pairs an NLP claim-extraction pipeline, built on ClimateBERT (Webersinke et al., 2022) and the environmental claims framework of Stammbach et al. (2022), with an independent satellite measurement built on the Hansen Global Forest Change dataset (Hansen et al., 2013) and modelled on PalmWatch's mill-buffer method. The two tracks are built separately and combined only at the final scoring step, so neither influences the other (Section 3.1). This is what makes the resulting mismatch score a genuine check rather than a self-confirming loop, and it is the specific design response to the missing verification step that Calamai et al. and Ong et al. both describe.

## 7.3 Policy implications

The EU Deforestation Regulation, 2023/1115, sets 31 December 2020 as the date after which deforestation-linked commodities are excluded from the EU market, regardless of whether the clearing was legal where it occurred (Section 2.4). Because this thesis measures loss from that same date, its results speak directly to what EUDR due-diligence enforcement will need to contend with (Section 5.5).

Two implications follow. First, since post-2020 loss appears near every mill in this 11-company sample, due-diligence checks built around flagging a small set of bad actors are starting from the wrong premise. The condition the thesis set out to check for is not rare; it is the baseline. Second, the Astra Agro case shows that a company's own disclosures are shaped by how much it chooses to say. A company that makes few, vague public claims is not necessarily lower risk. It is simply harder to check against its own words using a claims-contradiction method. Regulators and downstream buyers who treat claim volume or confidence as a screening signal should weight it only weakly, and should give more weight to independent, satellite-derived measures of the kind built in this thesis than to claim language alone.

## 7.4 Future work

Several directions follow from the limitations documented in Chapter 6. The Prithvi-EO-2.0 comparison in this thesis covers one mill, IOI's SYARIMO site, and returned a moderate positive correlation with the Hansen ground truth (Spearman 0.597). Scaling this comparison across more of the 290 mills, and across a wider range of vegetation types and clearing patterns, would show whether a frozen foundation model's change signal generalizes beyond a single site.

The 10 km buffer used throughout this thesis is a deliberate simplification of PalmWatch's road-network-refined catchment method, chosen to keep the method reproducible across 290 mills without individually verified road data (Section 3.3.2). A sensitivity analysis testing how the mismatch ranking shifts under different buffer radii, or under a road-network-refined boundary for at least a subset of mills, would show how much of the ranking depends on this particular methodological choice.

The external validation note checked four companies, GAR, IOI, SD Guthrie, and Astra Agro, against RSPO, Forest Peoples Programme, and Mongabay reporting. Two showed good directional agreement, one showed a plausible but not strongly confirming mid-table match, and one, Astra Agro, showed a clear tension that exposed a structural property of the scoring method. Extending this validation to the remaining seven companies would test how far that pattern holds across the full dataset, and would help establish whether the Astra Agro case is an isolated exception or a more general feature of how claims-contradiction scoring treats companies that say little.

Finally, the low-n claim samples for KLK and Wilmar (Section 6.2) would benefit from additional claim extraction, drawing on a longer history of each company's sustainability reports rather than the single most recent report used here. A larger sample would make the specificity and sentiment components of the mismatch score more stable for the companies where they currently rest on the fewest observations.

This thesis does not claim to have built a legal instrument for proving greenwashing. It has built a method, grounded in open satellite data and public company reports, for placing a company's language against its measured footprint and showing where the two agree and where they do not. That placement, and the honesty required to report it even when it complicates the story, as the Astra Agro case does, is the contribution this thesis offers.
