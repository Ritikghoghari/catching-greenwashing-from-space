# Abstract

Palm oil companies routinely publish zero-deforestation pledges, but no established method checks these pledges against independent evidence. Prior work either scores corporate climate language for vagueness or measures forest loss by satellite, without connecting the two. Recent surveys identify this missing verification step as an open gap in greenwashing detection research: text-based tools can flag vague claims but cannot confirm whether the underlying facts are true.

This thesis closes that gap for palm oil by combining two independently built evidence streams. An NLP pipeline, using ClimateBERT and an environmental-claims classification framework, extracts and scores 55 deforestation-related claims from the sustainability reports of 11 major companies for specificity and sentiment. A satellite pipeline, using the Hansen Global Forest Change dataset within a 10 km buffer around 290 matched mills, measures forest loss after 31 December 2020, the compliance cutoff set by the EU Deforestation Regulation. The two streams combine only at a final scoring step into a four-component mismatch score, so neither influences the other.

The satellite record shows 890,168 hectares of post-2020 forest loss across the 290 mills, with every single mill recording measurable loss. Golden Agri-Resources shows the largest total loss, 171,236 hectares, while IOI shows the highest per-mill intensity, 19 percent of buffer forest area lost. The mismatch score ranks KLK highest, driven by highly specific claims set against a substantial loss record, and Astra Agro lowest, not because its loss is small but because its public claims are few and vague, giving the method little to contradict despite an independently documented history of serious violations.

Forest loss near palm oil mills is not confined to a handful of poorly performing companies but is present across the entire sample, with real variation in how public language corresponds to it. This has direct relevance for EU Deforestation Regulation due-diligence enforcement, which cannot rely on claim volume or confidence as a screening signal. The thesis concludes that pairing satellite measurement with claim extraction offers a reproducible way to verify corporate environmental claims, while noting the method measures correspondence between language and evidence, not legal proof of causation.
