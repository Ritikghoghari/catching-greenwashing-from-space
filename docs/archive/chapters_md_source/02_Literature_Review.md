# Chapter 2: Literature Review

## 2.1 Greenwashing and Corporate Vague Language

Greenwashing is the practice of making a company appear more environmentally responsible than its actual conduct supports. In the palm oil sector, the most common form of this is the zero-deforestation pledge: a public commitment stating that a company's supply chain no longer causes forest loss. These pledges are now standard across major producers. The question this thesis asks is whether the pledges hold up against independent evidence.

Bingler, Kraus, and Leippold (2022) give this problem a name that is useful throughout this thesis: cheap talk. Working with corporate climate disclosures, they show that companies frequently make climate-related statements that sound substantive but commit the company to nothing measurable. A sentence like "we are committed to sustainable palm oil" carries no target, no date, and no way to check whether it has been met. Bingler et al. also document cherry-picking: companies selectively disclose the risks and topics that reflect well on them, while omitting or softening less favourable information. Their central finding is that vagueness is not incidental. It is a measurable property of the text, and it correlates with weaker underlying performance. Companies whose disclosures lean toward vague, unfalsifiable language tend to be the same companies whose actual climate risk exposure is harder to verify by other means.

This finding matters for the current thesis in two ways. First, it establishes that vague language is not just a stylistic feature of corporate reporting. It is a signal that can be measured and used as a variable, not dismissed as noise. Second, it supports treating claim specificity as a graded property rather than a binary one. A company either does or does not claim zero deforestation, but the strength of that claim, whether it names a date, a verification method, a specific set of mills, or none of these, varies continuously. Chapter 3 builds directly on this distinction: claims extracted from sustainability reports are scored for specificity, following the hard-versus-soft claim split that Bingler et al. establish as a measurable category rather than a subjective judgment call.

Bingler et al. work with climate disclosure broadly, not palm oil specifically, and not deforestation claims specifically. Their dataset is built from general corporate climate reporting across sectors. This thesis narrows their finding to a single sector and a single, checkable claim: zero deforestation. That narrowing is what allows the vague-language signal to be paired with a satellite ground truth, which is not something Bingler et al. attempt. Their contribution stops at identifying and scoring vagueness in text. It does not ask whether the underlying claim, vague or specific, is actually true.

## 2.2 NLP for ESG and Climate Claim Extraction

Once vague language is established as a measurable category, the next problem is finding claims in text at scale. Sustainability reports run to hundreds of pages. Reading every sentence by hand does not scale across eleven companies and their full report histories, so this thesis relies on natural language processing to do the first pass of extraction.

Two papers form the technical basis for this. Webersinke, Kraus, Leippold, and Webersinke (2022) introduce ClimateBERT, a language model pretrained on a large corpus of climate-related corporate text, including sustainability reports, earnings calls, and press releases. The core argument for pretraining a domain-specific model rather than using a general-purpose one is vocabulary and phrasing. Corporate climate disclosure has its own conventions: standardised phrases, boilerplate policy language, sector-specific terms like "fresh fruit bunch" or "RSPO-certified," and reporting formats that a general text model has not seen enough of during its own training. ClimateBERT is trained on this domain specifically, which improves its ability to recognise climate-related sentences that a general model would miss or misclassify. Webersinke et al. report that this domain adaptation produces measurable gains on climate-text classification tasks over general-purpose baselines.

Stammbach, Webersinke, Bingler, Kraus, and Leippold (2022) build on this base model to construct a labelled dataset specifically for detecting environmental claims. They annotate 2,647 sentences drawn from sustainability reports, earnings calls, and annual reports, each labelled for whether the sentence makes a genuine environmental claim or merely mentions an environmental topic without asserting anything. This distinction is the working definition of a claim used throughout this thesis: a sentence like "we achieved zero deforestation across our supply base in 2023" is a claim, while a sentence like "deforestation is a global challenge facing the industry" is not, even though both sentences contain the word deforestation. Stammbach et al. train a classifier on this labelled dataset and show it can separate genuine claims from incidental mentions with reasonable accuracy, using ClimateBERT as the underlying model.

Together, these two papers give this thesis a working pipeline: a domain-pretrained model (Webersinke et al.) fine-tuned on a claim-detection task (Stammbach et al.). Chapter 3 applies this same combination directly. Candidate sentences are pulled from the eleven companies' sustainability reports using keyword filtering targeted at deforestation-related vocabulary, following the term list Stammbach et al. use to build their own dataset, and the resulting candidates are passed through the ClimateBERT claim classifier to confirm they assert something concrete rather than merely mention the topic.

What neither paper does is check the claims against anything outside the text. Stammbach et al.'s contribution ends at classification: a sentence is or is not a claim. Their dataset does not link claims to any external record of whether the claim is true. This is not a shortcoming of their work; it was not their research question. But it leaves a gap that this thesis is built to fill. Extracting a claim and confirming it is a genuine claim, rather than incidental text, is a necessary first step. It is not verification. Section 2.5 returns to this gap directly, after the satellite and regulatory background needed to close it is established.

## 2.3 Satellite-Based Deforestation Monitoring

The other half of the evidence this thesis needs is an independent, physical record of forest change. This cannot come from text. It has to come from direct observation of the land.

Hansen, Potapov, Moore, Hancher, Turubanova, Tyukavina, Thau, Stehman, Goetz, Loveland, Kommareddy, Egorov, Chini, Justice, and Townshend (2013), published in Science, produced the first global, annually resolved map of forest cover change using Landsat satellite imagery. Their dataset covers the entire land surface at 30-metre pixel resolution, with each pixel of detected tree cover loss assigned to the specific year the loss was recorded, starting in 2001 and updated annually since. This annual resolution is what makes the dataset usable for a before-and-after comparison at any chosen cutoff date, which is exactly how this thesis uses it in Chapter 3: every hectare of loss is dated, so loss before and after 2020 can be separated cleanly for each mill.

Hansen et al.'s dataset, commonly called the UMD Global Forest Change dataset, has become the standard reference dataset in remote sensing research on deforestation, and it is the dataset most independent monitoring organisations use as their ground truth, including PalmWatch, described below. Its main limitation, which Hansen et al. themselves note, is that it measures tree cover loss, not deforestation in a strict legal or ecological sense. A pixel is flagged as loss whether the cause is logging, fire, storm damage, or conversion to agricultural or plantation land, and the dataset does not distinguish between these causes on its own. This means a raw loss figure cannot, by itself, say whether the land is now growing oil palm, or whether it burned and was never replanted, or whether it was cleared and abandoned. This limitation carries through directly into Chapter 3 and is treated there as an explicit constraint on what the satellite layer of this thesis can and cannot claim.

PalmWatch, a monitoring initiative from Inclusive Development International, applies the Hansen dataset specifically to the palm oil sector. PalmWatch's method draws a catchment boundary around each palm oil mill, approximating the geographic area from which the mill sources its fresh fruit bunches, and then overlays the Hansen loss data within that boundary to produce a mill-level deforestation score. PalmWatch refines its catchment boundaries using road-network analysis, which brings the boundary closer to a mill's actual supply-chain footprint than a simple radius would.

PalmWatch is the closest existing methodology to the satellite layer of this thesis, and it is the direct model for the mill-buffer approach used in Chapter 3. It is also the source of a limitation this thesis inherits rather than solves. PalmWatch states plainly that forest loss occurring inside a mill's catchment cannot be attributed with certainty to that specific mill's own sourcing. Independent smallholders, other companies' supply chains, and unrelated land-use change can all occur inside the same catchment area. The catchment method measures spatial proximity between a mill and forest loss. It does not, on its own, establish that the mill caused the loss. This attribution limitation is not a flaw unique to PalmWatch's design; it is a structural limit of any buffer-based method that does not have parcel-level land ownership and supply-chain data for every hectare inside the buffer. This thesis uses a simplified fixed 10 km radius rather than PalmWatch's road-network-refined boundary, a deliberate simplification made to keep the method reproducible across 290 mills without individually verified road data, and this thesis carries forward the same attribution limitation PalmWatch documents for its own method.

What PalmWatch does not do is bring a company's own stated claims into the comparison. PalmWatch reports what the satellite record shows for a brand's supply chain. It does not extract or score what that brand has publicly said about its own deforestation performance, and it does not compute any measure of the gap between the two. That is the specific space this thesis occupies, combining PalmWatch's satellite method with the claim-extraction methods described in Section 2.2, which PalmWatch's own design does not include.

## 2.4 EUDR Regulatory Context

The two research traditions described so far, claim extraction and satellite monitoring, exist somewhat independently of any specific legal deadline. Regulation gives this thesis a reason to treat one particular date as decisive: 31 December 2020.

Regulation (EU) 2023/1115, the EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR), sets rules for commodities placed on the EU market, palm oil among them. Under EUDR, a commodity is treated as non-compliant if it is linked to land that was deforested after 31 December 2020, regardless of whether the deforestation itself was legal under the laws of the country where it occurred. This is a meaningful design choice in the regulation. It does not ask whether a company followed local law. It asks whether the land was forest as of a fixed date, and whether it stopped being forest after that date due to the commodity supply chain in question.

This cutoff date gives this thesis a defensible, externally grounded reference point rather than an arbitrary one. Companies in the dataset made most of their current zero-deforestation commitments in the years leading up to and following 2020. Splitting forest loss at this same date separates historical land clearing, which a company might reasonably argue predates its current policy, from clearing that occurred after the company's public commitments were already in place and after the date regulators themselves will use to assess compliance. Chapter 3 applies this cutoff to every mill in the dataset, and the headline figures of this thesis, 890,168 hectares of post-2020 forest loss across 290 mills, are a direct product of this regulatory anchor. Without EUDR's stated date, choosing any single cutoff year would be a methodological judgment call open to challenge. With it, the cutoff is the same one palm oil buyers in the EU market will actually be held to.

EUDR is a regulatory instrument, not a research method. It does not itself measure forest loss, and it does not extract or evaluate corporate claims. Its function in this thesis is narrower and more specific: it supplies the date that makes the claim-versus-satellite comparison meaningful in a regulatory sense, not just a descriptive one.

## 2.5 Research Gap

The three threads above, cheap talk in corporate disclosure, NLP claim extraction, and satellite-based deforestation monitoring, have so far been developed largely in parallel. Two recent papers make this separation explicit and identify it as an open problem in the greenwashing detection literature.

Calamai, Balalau, Le Guenedal, and Suchanek (2026) survey the NLP methods researchers currently use to approximate greenwashing detection in corporate text. Their survey covers a range of tasks: topic detection, stance detection, deceptive-pattern identification, and claim classification of the kind Stammbach et al. build. Calamai et al.'s central finding is a limitation shared across nearly all of this work: no dataset of verified greenwashing cases currently exists. Researchers can train a model to detect vague language, or to classify whether a sentence makes a claim, but they cannot train or evaluate a model against ground-truth labels of which companies actually greenwashed and which did not, because no such labelled dataset has been built. Calamai et al. argue that closing this gap requires more than a larger or better-tuned text classifier. It requires a principled way of connecting disclosure text to some form of external, independent verification.

Ong, Ni, and Bansal (2025) make a related and more specific point. Reviewing existing automated greenwashing detection tools, they find that verification, checking whether a stated claim corresponds to an outside, independently measured fact, is consistently missing. Existing tools score text for vagueness, sentiment, or topic consistency. None of them, in Ong et al.'s review, take a specific factual claim made in a report and check it against an independent data source that exists outside the text itself. A model can flag a sentence as vague or evasive. It cannot, using text alone, tell you whether the underlying fact the sentence describes, in this case, whether a specific company's mills experienced forest loss, is actually true. Ong et al. identify this missing verification step as the central novelty gap in the field: the tools that exist are good at reading what companies say, and have no mechanism for checking it against what companies do.

Read together, Calamai et al. and Ong et al. describe the same gap from two directions. Calamai et al. describe it at the level of the field: there is no verified-case dataset to build against. Ong et al. describe it at the level of the tool: existing systems have no verification mechanism at all. Both point to the same missing piece, an independent, non-textual signal that a claim can be checked against, rather than a bigger or better classifier applied to the same text.

This is precisely the gap the satellite layer described in Section 2.3 is positioned to fill. The Hansen dataset and PalmWatch's mill-level application of it are not built as a companion to any NLP claim-detection system. They exist as an independent measurement of forest cover, built from Landsat imagery, with no dependency on what any company has said in a report. Pairing this independent physical signal with the NLP claim-extraction pipeline described in Section 2.2 answers Calamai et al.'s call for principled verification and Ong et al.'s specific diagnosis of a missing verification step, without requiring a labelled dataset of confirmed greenwashing cases, which does not exist and cannot be built from text classification alone. The satellite record substitutes for the missing ground-truth labels: instead of asking a model to learn what greenwashing looks like from examples that do not exist, this thesis checks each claim against a forest-loss measurement that is independently verifiable regardless of what the claim says.

## 2.6 This Thesis's Novelty

The contribution of this thesis follows directly from the gap identified in Section 2.5. No existing tool, to the knowledge gathered in this review, combines NLP-based extraction of a company's own deforestation claims with satellite-based measurement of that same company's actual forest-loss footprint, for the palm oil sector specifically.

Stammbach et al. and Webersinke et al. build the claim-extraction side but stop at classification; they do not check claims against outside evidence. Bingler et al. score how vague a claim is but do not measure whether the underlying fact is true. Hansen et al. and PalmWatch build the satellite side but do not read or score what companies say about themselves; PalmWatch's catchment scores exist independently of any company statement. Calamai et al. and Ong et al. both identify the resulting gap in general terms, a missing verification step in greenwashing detection research, without proposing or building a specific tool that closes it for any one sector.

This thesis closes that gap for palm oil specifically, and does so at a scale that has not, to the knowledge gathered here, been attempted before: eleven companies, 290 matched mills, 55 extracted deforestation claims, checked directly against 890,168 hectares of post-2020 forest loss measured independently through satellite data. The design is deliberately two-track, following the separation argued for in Chapter 3: the claim-extraction layer and the satellite-measurement layer are built independently, so that neither influences the other, and the two are only brought together at a final mismatch-scoring step. This separation is what allows the comparison to function as a genuine check rather than a self-confirming loop. A company's claim score does not shape how its forest loss is measured, and its measured forest loss does not shape how its claims are read.

The resulting picture, eleven companies publicly stating zero-deforestation commitments while the satellite record shows measurable, dated, post-commitment forest loss across their matched mill networks, is the empirical core of this thesis. Where GAR shows the largest absolute post-2020 loss at 171,236 hectares across 50 mills, and IOI shows the highest per-mill loss intensity at 19 percent of buffer forest area, averaging 4,959 hectares per mill, the value of this thesis's approach is that these figures can be placed directly against each company's own public claims, rather than read as satellite data alone or as claim data alone. That pairing, not either evidence stream on its own, is the contribution this literature review has been building toward.
