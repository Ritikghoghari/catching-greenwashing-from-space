# Chapter 5: Discussion

## 5.1 What the results mean

The mismatch score in Section 4.3 was built to answer a specific question: does a company's public language about deforestation line up with what satellite data records near its mills. The ranking in Table 4.2 gives an answer, and the answer is not simple. No company scores near zero, and no company scores near the maximum. All eleven fall in a band between 17.6 and 65.6. Read together with the case studies in Sections 4.4 to 4.6, this spread says the gap between claim and evidence is not a story about a few bad actors sitting apart from an otherwise clean industry. It is a gradient. Every company in the dataset makes deforestation-related claims, every company's mills show post-2020 forest loss (Section 4.2), and the degree to which the two connect varies by company rather than being present or absent.

This matters for how the finding should be framed. A binary result, some companies greenwash and some do not, would be easier to state but would not match what the data shows. The more accurate claim is that the sector as a whole produces language that sits somewhere on a spectrum from closely tracking its own measured impact to sitting largely apart from it, and this thesis's contribution is a method for placing a company on that spectrum using public reports and open satellite data rather than requiring insider information.

## 5.2 Size vs intensity

Section 4.2 separates two ways of measuring forest loss: total hectares and per-mill intensity. GAR has the largest total, 171,236 ha across 50 mills. IOI has the highest intensity, 4,959 ha per mill and 19.04% of its forest_2000 baseline (Section 4.2). Neither is ranked first on the mismatch score. GAR is second. IOI is fourth.

This is not a flaw in the score. It is the point of building a composite measure instead of ranking by raw tonnage. A ranking based only on hectares lost would just reproduce the loss table already in Section 4.2, with mill count and company size doing most of the work. Larger companies with more mills will tend to show more total hectares of loss simply because they operate more sites, and companies with more concentrated operations will tend to show higher per-mill intensity. Neither pattern says anything about what the company is telling the public.

The mismatch score adds two components that raw loss data cannot supply: how specific a company's claims are, and how positively they read (Section 4.3). KLK ranks first not because its forest loss is the worst, its forest loss score of 33.9 is mid-pack, but because its claims score the maximum possible specificity, 100.0. GAR ranks second in large part because its claims carry the highest sentiment score in the dataset, 91.6, on top of a genuinely large loss footprint. The score is answering a different question than "which company clears the most forest." It is answering "which company's language most confidently commits to something that its own mill network's satellite record does not support." Those are related questions but they are not the same question, and the ranking difference between Sections 4.2 and 4.3 is exactly where that distinction becomes visible.

## 5.3 The spatial severity finding

One result in Section 4.2 applies to the entire dataset without exception: all 290 matched mills, across all 11 companies, show measurable post-2020 forest loss within their 10 km buffers. The smallest single-mill total is still 105 ha. No mill in this sample records zero loss.

This universality is worth sitting with. It means that at the scale this thesis measures, near-mill forest loss since the EUDR cutoff is not a problem confined to a handful of poorly performing companies. It is present everywhere the pipeline looked. That does not mean every mill bears equal responsibility, Section 3.3.2 and Section 5.6 below discuss why buffer-based measurement cannot establish causal attribution, but it does mean that the underlying condition the thesis set out to check for, forest loss near palm oil infrastructure, is not a rare event in this sample. It is the baseline.

Against that baseline, the severity share measure in Section 4.2 shows real variation in how that loss is distributed within a company's mill network. IOI has 80% of its mills classified as severe, exceeding the dataset-wide median loss (12 of 15). SIPEF has 27% (3 of 11). Both companies operate inside a sector where every mill shows some loss, but IOI's loss pattern is concentrated across most of its network, while SIPEF's is concentrated in a minority of sites. This distinction goes beyond what a single total-hectares figure can show. Two companies with similar total loss could arrive at that total in very different ways, one from a handful of extreme sites and the rest of the network relatively quiet, another from most sites showing moderate, ongoing clearing. The severity share is a way of telling those two patterns apart, and it feeds into the mismatch score for that reason (Section 4.3).

## 5.4 Comparison against external validation

The validation note (docs/chapters/validation_note.md) checked four companies against sources published independently of this thesis, RSPO Complaints Panel records, Forest Peoples Programme statements, and Mongabay reporting. Two of the four checks line up well with the mismatch score. GAR's rank 2 position sits next to an active RSPO complaint alleging more than 75,000 ha of unlawful oil palm production inside Indonesia's protected Forest Zone, and a 2023 withdrawal from the High Carbon Stock Approach, the industry's shared no-deforestation standard. A company with an unresolved land-use complaint of that scale, and a public exit from a shared deforestation commitment, while still making zero-deforestation claims, is consistent with a high mismatch score. IOI's documented 2016 RSPO suspension for clearing rainforest without permits, followed by a public NDPE commitment, also matches the general shape the score is designed to catch, though that specific evidence predates the thesis's post-2020 measurement window.

The fourth check, Astra Agro, does not line up, and this is the most important result in this chapter. Astra Agro ranks lowest of the 11 companies on the mismatch score, 17.6. Independent reporting from Mongabay, based on a Genesis Bengkulu geospatial investigation, documents roughly 17,664 ha of Astra Agro subsidiary concessions overlapping forest zones, including about 1,100 ha inside protected conservation forest, three subsidiaries operating without required land-use permits, and a land dispute affecting more than 6,700 ha without free, prior and informed consent from local communities, with reports of intimidation as recent as December 2023. Civil society groups publicly opposed the company's 2024 RSPO membership application on greenwashing grounds. None of this reads as a company with a clean environmental record. It reads as one of the more serious cases in the sector.

The mechanism behind this gap is visible in the score's own components. Astra Agro's forest loss is real and substantial, 87,198 ha, the fifth-largest total in the dataset (Section 4.2). What pulls its mismatch score down is not the loss figure. It is that Astra Agro's specificity score is 13.8, second-lowest in the dataset, and its sentiment score is 0.0, the floor of the normalised scale (Section 4.6). The company simply does not make many strong, specific, confidently-worded public commitments about deforestation. It states less, and states it more flatly, than GAR or SD Guthrie do. A claims-contradiction score can only measure the size of the gap between what a company says and what satellite data shows. When a company says relatively little, and says it without much conviction, there is less for the satellite record to contradict, regardless of how severe that record actually is.

This is not a data error. The forest loss figures for Astra Agro are the same figures used for every other company, drawn from the same Hansen dataset (Hansen et al., 2013) and the same buffer method. The arithmetic behind the mismatch score is sound. What this result exposes is a property of the framing itself: a score built to measure contradiction between claims and evidence will structurally favor companies that talk less, because a quiet company gives the method less to work with, independent of how much harm its operations actually cause. This is a genuine finding, not a flaw to explain away. It means the mismatch score should be read as exactly what it measures, correspondence between public language and satellite evidence, and not as a general severity ranking of environmental harm. Astra Agro's case demonstrates that those two things can point in opposite directions, and a reader using this method to judge which company is worse, in an absolute sense, would be misled by the ranking alone. The raw loss figures in Table 4.1 need to sit alongside the mismatch ranking for exactly this reason.

## 5.5 EUDR policy relevance

The EU Deforestation Regulation, 2023/1115, sets 31 December 2020 as the cutoff date after which commodity production linked to deforestation or forest degradation is excluded from the EU market. This thesis measures forest loss from that same date, so its results speak directly to what EUDR due-diligence enforcement will need to contend with.

Two findings matter here. First, the universal presence of post-2020 loss across all 290 mills (Section 4.2, Section 5.3) means that due-diligence checks limited to a handful of flagged suppliers will miss most of the picture. If every mill in an 11-company sample shows measurable loss since the cutoff, an enforcement approach built around identifying a small set of bad actors is starting from the wrong premise. Second, the Astra Agro tension in Section 5.4 is directly relevant to how due-diligence statements should be read. A company's own sustainability disclosures, the kind EUDR compliance will partly rely on, are shaped by how much a company chooses to claim. A company that says little may not be safer. It may simply be harder to check against its own words. Regulators and downstream buyers relying on self-reported claims as a screening signal should treat claim volume and confidence as, at best, weakly related to underlying risk, and should weight satellite-derived, independent measures like the ones in this thesis more heavily than claim language alone.

## 5.6 Limitations

Several limitations bear directly on how these results should be used.

The buffer method measures forest loss inside a 10 km catchment around each mill, following PalmWatch's own approach and its documented limitation (Section 3.3.2). Loss inside a buffer is not proof that a specific mill's supply chain caused that loss. Land inside a 10 km radius can be cleared for reasons unconnected to the mill, by other actors, for other crops, or for reasons unrelated to palm oil entirely. The universal-loss finding in Section 5.3 should be read as loss occurring near mill infrastructure at scale, not as loss definitively attributable to each mill's own operations. This is the same limitation PalmWatch names for its own methodology, and this thesis inherits it by design rather than by oversight.

Claim-count varies sharply across companies, and this affects how much confidence to place in specificity and sentiment means for the smaller samples. KLK's mean specificity score, the input that puts it in first place on the mismatch ranking, is built from only 8 claims (Section 4.1, Section 4.3), flagged in the underlying data as a low-n case. Wilmar's claim sample is smaller still. A mean drawn from 4 to 8 claims can be shifted substantially by one or two unusually worded sentences in a way that a 40-claim sample like GAR's, or a 66-claim sample like IOI's, cannot. The KLK result in particular should be treated as suggestive rather than settled, and would benefit from a larger claim sample in future extraction work.

The Astra Agro finding in Section 5.4 is itself a limitation of the method, not only a result. Any company that makes few, low-conviction public claims will tend to score low on this formula regardless of the severity of its actual environmental record, because the score measures contradiction, not harm. This is worth restating plainly here: the mismatch score is not a general-purpose severity ranking, and should not be presented as one without the qualification this section provides.

Finally, the Prithvi-EO-2.0 proof of concept in Section 4.7 covers exactly one mill, IOI's SYARIMO site, chosen because it is IOI's highest-loss location. A single-mill comparison, even one returning a moderate positive correlation with the Hansen ground truth (Spearman 0.597, p < 0.0001), cannot establish that a frozen foundation model's change signal would generalize across the other 289 mills in this dataset, across different vegetation types, cloud conditions, or clearing patterns. It is offered in Chapter 4 as corroborating evidence for the primary Hansen-based pipeline, and that is the limit of what one mill can support.
