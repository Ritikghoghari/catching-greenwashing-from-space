# Chapter 1: Introduction

## 1.1 The greenwashing problem in palm oil

Palm oil production is one of the most closely watched drivers of tropical deforestation. In response, most major producers now publish public zero-deforestation commitments: statements that their supply chains, from plantation to mill, no longer contribute to forest loss. These pledges appear in sustainability reports, on company websites, and in investor communications, and they are now standard practice across the sector rather than an exception.

The problem this thesis addresses is that a pledge is not evidence. A company can state that it has achieved zero deforestation without that statement being checked against anything outside the report itself. Bingler, Kraus, and Leippold (2022) describe this pattern in corporate climate disclosure more broadly as cheap talk: statements that sound substantive but commit the company to nothing measurable, with no target, no date, and no way to verify whether the claim has been met. Their work also shows that vagueness is not incidental. It is a measurable property of a company's language, and companies whose disclosures lean vague tend to be the same companies whose actual performance is hardest to verify by other means (Section 2.1). In the palm oil sector specifically, a zero-deforestation claim is exactly the kind of statement this pattern applies to: it is checkable in principle, because forest cover can be observed directly from satellite imagery, but it is rarely checked in practice.

## 1.2 The verification gap

Two research traditions have developed largely in parallel to address pieces of this problem, but neither closes it on its own.

The first is natural language processing applied to corporate climate disclosure. Webersinke et al. (2022) built ClimateBERT, a language model pretrained on climate-related corporate text, to better recognise the vocabulary and phrasing of sustainability reporting. Stammbach et al. (2022) built on this to construct a labelled dataset for detecting genuine environmental claims, as distinct from sentences that merely mention an environmental topic without asserting anything. Together these tools can find and classify a claim at scale. What they cannot do is say whether the claim is true, because their datasets do not link any extracted claim to an external, independent record (Section 2.2).

The second tradition is satellite-based forest monitoring. Hansen et al. (2013) produced the first global, annually resolved map of forest cover change from Landsat imagery, now the standard reference dataset in remote sensing research on deforestation. PalmWatch, a monitoring initiative from Inclusive Development International, applies this dataset specifically to the palm oil sector, drawing a catchment boundary around each mill and measuring forest loss inside it. This gives an independent physical record of what has happened on the ground. But PalmWatch's method does not read or score what a company has actually claimed about its own performance. It reports the satellite record on its own, without a corresponding claim to compare it against (Section 2.3).

Two recent surveys name the resulting gap directly. Calamai, Balalau, Le Guenedal, and Suchanek (2026) find that no dataset of verified greenwashing cases currently exists, so NLP models can be trained to detect vague language but cannot be evaluated against ground truth of which companies actually greenwashed. Ong, Ni, and Bansal (2025) find the same gap described from the tool side: existing automated greenwashing detectors score text for vagueness or sentiment, but none of them check a specific factual claim against an independent, non-textual data source. Both papers describe the same missing piece from different angles: an independent signal that a claim can be checked against, rather than a better classifier applied to the same text (Section 2.5).

The Hansen dataset and PalmWatch's mill-level application of it already exist as exactly this kind of independent signal. What is missing is a method that pairs them with a claim-extraction pipeline built to the same rigor, for a specific sector, at a scale large enough to produce a company-level result. That is the space this thesis occupies.

Closing this gap does not require a labelled dataset of confirmed greenwashing cases, which does not exist and cannot be built from text classification alone, as Calamai et al. note. It requires substituting the satellite record for the missing ground-truth labels: instead of asking a model to learn what greenwashing looks like from examples that do not exist, each claim can be checked directly against a forest-loss measurement that is independently verifiable regardless of what the claim itself says (Section 2.5). This is the specific design choice this thesis makes, and it is what separates the approach taken here from a text-only classifier, however well trained.

## 1.3 Approach

This thesis combines two independently built evidence streams and compares them at a final scoring step, rather than building a single model that tries to detect greenwashing from text alone.

The first stream is textual. An NLP pipeline, using ClimateBERT and the environmental-claims framework of Stammbach et al. (2022), extracts deforestation-related claims from the sustainability reports of 11 major palm oil companies. Each extracted claim is scored for specificity, following Bingler et al.'s distinction between claims that name a concrete target, date, or verification method and claims that use only general commitment language, and separately for sentiment, capturing how confidently or positively the claim is worded (Section 3.2).

The second stream is physical. A satellite pipeline, using the Hansen Global Forest Change dataset accessed through Google Earth Engine, measures forest loss within a 10 km buffer around each of 290 mills matched to the 11 companies via the Universal Mill List. Loss is split at 31 December 2020, the cutoff date set by the EU Deforestation Regulation, Regulation (EU) 2023/1115, under which a commodity is treated as non-compliant if it is linked to deforestation after this date regardless of local legality (Section 3.3, Section 3.4).

The two streams are built without reference to each other, so that an error or bias in one cannot quietly shape the other. They are combined only in a final mismatch score: a four-component, 0-100 measure weighting forest loss, claim specificity, claim sentiment, and the share of a company's mills showing severe loss (Section 3.5, Section 4.3). A high mismatch score identifies a company whose language commits confidently to something specific, positioned next to a satellite record that does not support it. A low score does not mean a clean record; it can also mean a company that says little, a distinction this thesis treats as a central finding rather than a footnote (Chapter 5, Chapter 6).

One finding that emerges from the satellite layer before any claim-comparison is made is worth stating here because it frames what the mismatch scoring step is actually measuring: every one of the 290 mills in this dataset shows post-2020 forest loss. The minimum loss figure, for the least affected mill in the dataset, is 105 hectares. This is not a finding that distinguishes companies from each other; it is a finding that applies uniformly across all of them. A scoring method based on whether a mill shows any loss at all would assign the maximum possible score to every company, which is not useful. The mismatch score is therefore built not on whether loss occurred, but on the scale, spatial spread, and specificity context of that loss, compared against what each company has publicly claimed. This distinction, between a universal finding and a differentiating one, is central to how the results in Chapter 4 should be read.

## 1.4 Research questions

This thesis asks three related questions. First, do palm oil companies' public deforestation claims correspond to what satellite data shows near their own mills, and how much does this correspondence vary across companies. Second, does measuring correspondence this way produce a different picture than measuring forest loss alone, and if so, what does that difference reveal about how claims should be read. Third, what does an independently verifiable, reproducible method built on public reports and open satellite data contribute to the verification gap identified in the literature, and what are the limits of what such a method can claim to prove.

## 1.5 Thesis structure

Chapter 2 reviews the literature this thesis draws on: cheap talk in corporate disclosure, NLP claim extraction, satellite-based deforestation monitoring, the EUDR regulatory context, and the specific verification gap this thesis is built to fill. Chapter 3 documents the methodology in the order the pipeline runs: claim extraction, satellite measurement, mill matching, and the mismatch-scoring method, including the data-quality corrections made along the way. Chapter 4 presents the results: claim extraction outcomes, satellite forest-loss findings, the full mismatch-score ranking, and three case studies, GAR, IOI, and Astra Agro, that illustrate what the score does and does not capture, along with a supplementary Prithvi-EO-2.0 proof of concept. Chapter 5 discusses what these results mean, including the tension exposed by the Astra Agro case and the relevance of the findings to EUDR policy enforcement. Chapter 6 states the limitations of the method directly. Chapter 7 concludes with a summary of findings, the research gap this thesis fills, policy implications, and directions for future work.
